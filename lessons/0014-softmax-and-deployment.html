Lesson 0014 · Homework 1, Problem 3

Softmax, log loss, and the action after prediction

Softmax itself is already familiar from Lesson 0002. What's new here is doing the arithmetic by hand for one image, and reasoning about what the resulting probability vector should — and shouldn't — trigger downstream.

Why not just regress on the digit codes?

MNIST labels are the codes 0–9, but they're categories, not measurements — "7" isn't 7 units more of something than "0" the way $70 is more than $0. Fitting one scalar output directly to those codes with squared error would silently assume a false thing: that predicting 6 when the truth is 7 is a "small" error, and predicting 0 when the truth is 7 is a "big" one — as if the digits sat on a meaningful number line. They don't; digit 6 is not "closer" to digit 7 in any sense a squared-error loss should care about.

Softmax regression sidesteps this by giving each class its own logit and never comparing digits as numbers at all — see Lesson 0002 for the full softmax setup, and the glossary's logit / argmax entries.

Try it: from logits to probabilities

A parallel setup — four categories instead of ten, two of them with elevated logits, the rest flat at zero. Same shape as Homework 1's setup, different numbers.

With those starting sliders, the denominator is elog3 + e0 + elog5 + e0 = 3+1+5+1 = 10, giving probabilities 0.3, 0.1, 0.5, 0.1 — they sum to 1 (they always do, that's what normalizing means), and argmax picks class C.

For Homework 1(b): the actual logits are z1 = log 2, z3 = log 4, and zero for the other 8 classes — ten classes total, not four. Same denominator-then-divide procedure; different numbers and a different count of "flat" classes to fold together.

Log loss punishes low confidence in the truth — not wrongness in general

Suppose the true label is A here (probability 0.3), but the model's argmax pick was C. The model is wrong by the usual arg max rule. Its multiclass log loss on this image is −log(0.3) ≈ 1.204. Compare that to a uniform predictor that has no opinion at all and assigns 0.25 to every one of the 4 classes: −log(0.25) ≈ 1.386.

The probability vector is not the decision rule

Homework 1(d) adds a policy on top of the model: auto-enter a digit only when maxc pc(x) ≥ 0.80; otherwise route to a person. In the demo's numbers, max probability is 0.5 (class C) — below a threshold like 0.75, so this image would be sent for human review, not auto-entered, even though the model does have a definite argmax opinion.

This is the same distinction Lesson 0012 draws for the advising threshold: f^D produces the probability vector; δ is a separate, chosen rule layered on top that decides what to actually do with it. Two systems can use the identical fitted model and produce completely different real-world behavior just by setting the confidence threshold differently.

Evaluating for new writers — the same idea as Lesson 0012, again

"Represents new writers" is the same held-out design question as Problem 1(d): if your held-out images include handwriting from the same person who also contributed training images, you're not testing what happens on a genuinely new writer — you're testing partial recognition of a writer's own style. A held-out split should hold out entire writers, not just individual images, mirroring the grouped-by-student split from Lesson 0012.

And accuracy alone won't tell the deployed system's whole story — with a human-review threshold in the loop, useful additional numbers include the fraction of images routed to a human (too high, and the "automation" isn't automating much) and accuracy restricted to the auto-entered subset (the number that actually matters, since those are the ones nobody double-checks).
